# Flash Flood Early Warning — Deep Research Prompt Pack

Anchor event: Bhote Koshi / Trishuli flood, Nepal–Tibet border, 26 August 2026. Seven prompts, one per deep research run, each self-contained — paste into any deep research tool as-is. Suggested order: **1, 2, 6 first** (they establish what is physically detectable and what is exposed — everything else rests on that), then 3, 4, 5, and **7 last** (the prototype question only makes sense once the others are answered). Figures in the context blocks are provisional — the event is days old — so every prompt instructs the tool to re-verify them.

---

## Prompt 1 — Event forensics: what exactly happened, and was it detectable in advance?

```
You are researching the 26 August 2026 Bhote Koshi flash flood disaster on the
Nepal-Tibet border for someone designing an early warning system. Your job is
forensic reconstruction and a detectability verdict.

CONTEXT (reported facts as of 28 Aug 2026 — verify everything, figures were
still moving): A glacier section reportedly collapsed around 8:37-8:40am NPT
from roughly 5,000-5,200m elevation near Langtang Lirung (Rasuwa district).
The ice-rock avalanche dammed the Lhende Khola near the Chinese border; the
dam then burst, sending a debris flood into the Bhote Koshi and Trishuli
rivers through Rasuwa, Nuwakot, and Dhading. It was not raining. The flood
crossed the border at Rasuwagadhi and reportedly reached Syabrubesi (~14 km)
in about 10 minutes; the Trishuli reportedly rose ~9m in 30 minutes. The
seismic signal (magnitude reported between 4.4 and 5.2) was initially called
an earthquake; USGS later said the signal was generated by the landslide
itself, while Chinese authorities have maintained an earthquake trigger. The
same valley had a glacial outburst flood on 8 July 2025 (reportedly a
supraglacial lake breach) that destroyed the Nepal-China Friendship Bridge.

QUESTIONS:
1. Build the most authoritative minute-by-minute timeline available: collapse,
   damming, how long the dam held, breach, arrival times at each settlement
   and river gauge downstream, and when each warning action happened
   (the ~9:00am telephone notification to Nepal's Flood Forecasting Division,
   SMS dissemination from ~9:16am).
2. What do scientists conclude about the mechanism and trigger? Is the
   earthquake question settled? Prioritize primary expert commentary (Dave
   Petley's landslide blog at AGU/Eos, ICIMOD, USGS statements, Chinese
   Academy of Sciences researchers) over news summaries.
3. Precursors: was there a temperature anomaly in the preceding days? Any
   satellite-visible deformation, crevassing, or meltwater ponding before the
   collapse (Sentinel-1/2, Landsat, Planet)? Any reported pre-collapse
   rockfall or icefall activity? Had anyone flagged this slope or valley as
   dangerous after the July 2025 flood?
4. Compare with the 7 February 2021 Chamoli (Uttarakhand, India) rock-ice
   avalanche: what did post-event forensics (e.g., the Science paper by
   Shugar et al.) find about precursors that were visible in hindsight?
5. VERDICT: for this event type in this valley, what is the maximum realistic
   warning lead time with today's best practice — days (source monitoring of
   the unstable mass), tens of minutes (automatic seismic detection of the
   collapse), or single minutes (river sensors)? Be explicit about the
   uncertainty in this verdict.

OUTPUT: timeline table; mechanism summary that clearly marks disputed points;
precursor inventory with evidence quality; the lead-time verdict. Name every
source in full (organization + document title + publication date). Label each
claim as confirmed / single-source / disputed. Every casualty or damage figure
must carry an as-of date. Do not invent figures or sources; say plainly when
something is unknown.
```

## Prompt 2 — Detection at the source: the global state of the art

```
You are researching real-time detection technology for glacier collapses,
ice-rock avalanches, landslide dams, and glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs),
to assess what could be deployed in Nepal's Bhote Koshi-Trishuli corridor.

CONTEXT: In the 26 August 2026 Bhote Koshi disaster, the flood reached the
first town in ~10 minutes and all of Nepal's DHM river gauges in the corridor
(three or four stations; reports differ) were destroyed before crossing alert
thresholds — the first automated alert would have fired roughly three hours
after the event, far downstream. The hazard source sits at ~5,000m+ on the
Tibet side of the border. Detection must therefore happen at or near the
source, not downstream. It was not raining, so rainfall-based forecasting was
irrelevant.

QUESTIONS:
1. Seismic and infrasound detection: how do operational systems perform for
   debris flows and mass movements (Swiss/Alpine networks, Sichuan debris-flow
   monitoring in China, Alaska landslide work, volcano lahar detection systems)?
   Key metrics: detection latency, discrimination reliability (avalanche vs
   noise vs earthquake), false alarm rates, cost, and whether automated
   alerting within 1-3 minutes of a collapse is operationally proven anywhere.
2. Satellite: current realistic revisit and data-latency for SAR (Sentinel-1,
   ICEYE, Capella), optical, and thermal. Can any satellite service today
   deliver operational forewarning of slope/glacier instability (days ahead),
   or is it limited to after-action mapping? What does commercial tasking cost?
3. In-situ source monitoring: ground-based interferometric radar, GNSS
   stations, and tiltmeters on unstable ice/rock. Study the May 2025 Birch
   Glacier / Blatten (Valais, Switzerland) case where a village was evacuated
   before a glacier collapse: exactly what instrumentation, monitoring
   arrangement, cost, and institutional setup made that possible, and what
   preconditions (access, power, telecom, known hazard) it required.
4. Upstream tripwires: lake-level sensors and gauges high in the catchment
   (as used in GLOF systems in Nepal e.g. Tsho Rolpa, Bhutan, Peru
   e.g. Lake 513, Central Asia). Track record, survival rates, maintenance.
5. SYNTHESIS: what is the minimum viable detection architecture for the
   Bhote Koshi corridor that gives at least ~15 minutes of warning to
   Syabrubesi and ~1 hour to Trishuli-valley towns? Give rough cost bands
   (capex + annual opex) and name vendors/research groups who do this work.
   Include lessons from deployments that failed or decayed.

OUTPUT: a capability matrix (method x achievable lead time x latency x false
alarm rate x cost band x maintenance burden); a three-case comparison
(Blatten 2025 = warning succeeded; Chamoli 2021 = no warning; Bhote Koshi
2026 = no warning); a recommended shortlist architecture. Name every source
in full (organization + title + date). Mark vendor marketing claims as such.
State uncertainty explicitly rather than smoothing over it.
```

## Prompt 3 — The transboundary problem

```
You are researching transboundary early warning: what works when the hazard
source sits in one country and the deaths happen in another. The case is
China (Tibet) upstream, Nepal downstream.

CONTEXT: The 26 August 2026 Bhote Koshi flood originated on or near the
Tibetan side of the border. After a flood in the same valley in July 2025,
Nepal and China reportedly agreed to establish a bilateral mechanism for
sharing water-related risk data in border regions. In August 2026 no advance
warning crossed the border; Nepali officials said Chinese authorities were
caught off-guard too (there were casualties on the Chinese side), and China's
embassy publicly disputed the framing that China failed to warn Nepal.

QUESTIONS:
1. What exactly does the post-2025 Nepal-China understanding contain, per
   official statements from both governments — and is there any evidence it
   functioned at all in August 2026?
2. Case studies of transboundary flood/hazard warning that demonstrably
   function: the Mekong River Commission, the EU's EFAS and Danube
   arrangements, India-Nepal cooperation on the Kosi and Gandak, Central Asian
   GLOF cooperation, Alpine cross-border arrangements. For each: what is
   shared, at what latency, under what legal basis, and what made it work —
   treaty, technology, personal networks, or a third-party broker?
3. Failure cases: where transboundary warning broke down (e.g., data secrecy
   on the Brahmaputra, politicized hydrology between hostile neighbors) and
   the specific reasons.
4. Third-party workarounds that need no bilateral goodwill: ICIMOD's regional
   role, WMO systems and the UN Early Warnings for All initiative, satellite
   monitoring that requires no host-country consent, and any precedent of
   independent monitoring substituting for an uncooperative upstream state.
5. What could realistically get sensor data from the upper Lhende catchment
   (Chinese territory) feeding Nepali alerting within this decade? Are there
   precedents for foreign or joint instrumentation on Chinese territory, or
   for China sharing real-time (not seasonal) hydrological data with a
   neighbor?

OUTPUT: a comparative table of transboundary mechanisms (what is shared,
latency, legal basis, whether it survives political stress); an honest
assessment of the China-Nepal channel's near-term prospects; a ranked list of
warning pathways that do NOT depend on Chinese cooperation. Name every source
in full (organization + title + date). Separate official government positions
from journalistic characterization. Flag where the record is simply unknown.
```

## Prompt 4 — The last mile: dissemination and response at minutes scale

```
You are researching warning dissemination and protective response when lead
time is minutes to one hour, for Himalayan river-valley communities.

CONTEXT: In the 26 August 2026 Bhote Koshi flood (Nepal), the functioning
warning channel was a telephone call to the Flood Forecasting Division at
~9:00am — roughly 20 minutes after the flood entered Nepal — followed by mass
SMS from ~9:16am (reportedly ~679,000 messages via Nepal Telecom and Ncell),
plus calls, radio and Facebook. One school in Bidur reportedly received the
alert during morning assembly and evacuated ~1,650 students uphill. Upstream
communities got no warning at all. Settlements sit on narrow valley floors;
the population includes trekkers and tourists on foreign SIMs; hydropower
crews work in tunnels (hundreds were reportedly rescued from the Upper
Trishuli project tunnel).

QUESTIONS:
1. Cell broadcast vs SMS: latency and reach differences, behavior with
   foreign SIMs, and Nepal's actual status — does Nepal have cell broadcast
   capability or Common Alerting Protocol (CAP) adoption? What is the status
   of the UN Early Warnings for All rollout in Nepal?
2. Sirens: costs, power/telecom requirements, trigger chains, maintenance
   record in comparable settings, and evidence on effectiveness (Japanese
   tsunami practice, Indonesian experience, Alpine debris-flow sirens,
   existing GLOF sirens in Nepal such as the Tsho Rolpa system's history).
3. Community-based EWS: Nepal's Karnali-basin gauge-reader model (Practical
   Action and partners) — documented performance in real floods, costs, and
   decay rates. What keeps volunteer networks alive?
4. Special populations: what protocols made the Bidur school evacuation work,
   and what do best-practice school, hydropower-operator (tunnel evacuation),
   and trekking-lodge/tourism protocols look like? Any existing obligations
   on Nepali hydropower licensees to warn downstream?
5. Human factors at minutes scale: comprehension of warnings, trust and false
   alarm fatigue, night-time events, and vertical evacuation / safe-haven
   siting in gorge topography. What does evidence say about whether drills
   change outcomes?

OUTPUT: a ranked set of last-mile interventions for this corridor by
lives-saved potential per dollar, each with an evidence grade and named
implementers with track records. Name every source in full (organization +
title + date). Distinguish independently evaluated evidence from NGO
self-reporting. Do not present a plausible-sounding intervention as proven
if the evidence is thin — say so.
```

## Prompt 5 — Institutions, money, and why warning systems die

```
You are researching the institutional and financial side of early warning in
Nepal: who owns it, why the chain failed on 26 August 2026, what funding
exists, and why installed systems decay.

CONTEXT: In the August 2026 Bhote Koshi disaster, Nepal's Department of
Hydrology and Meteorology (DHM) reportedly learned of the flood by telephone
~16-20 minutes after it entered Nepal, via the Rasuwa District Administration
and a gauge-station staffer; the National Disaster Risk Reduction and
Management Authority (NDRRMA) was in the loop; mass SMS followed within
minutes of DHM knowing. The upstream gauges were destroyed before triggering.
The same corridor flooded in July 2025.

QUESTIONS:
1. Mandate map: under Nepali law and practice, who is responsible for hazard
   detection, alert decision, dissemination, and evacuation — DHM, NDRRMA,
   district administrations, security forces, municipalities, hydropower
   licensees? Where exactly did the chain have no owner on 26 August 2026?
2. The alert decision's political economy: who has authority to order
   evacuation, what does a false alarm cost them, and what does research say
   about designing trigger authority so officials actually pull the trigger?
3. Funding inventory: active early-warning / disaster-risk projects in Nepal
   from the World Bank, ADB, Green Climate Fund, bilateral donors, and Early
   Warnings for All commitments — name each project in full with amounts,
   dates, and what it actually pays for. Where does glacier/GLOF monitoring
   for the Rasuwa/Langtang region sit in current plans, especially post-July
   2025?
4. The decay problem: documented cases (Nepal and globally) of donor-installed
   warning systems going dark — sensor maintenance, telecom bills, staff
   turnover. What do evaluations (WMO, UNDRR, World Bank, academic) estimate
   about EWS failure/decay rates, and which governance designs keep systems
   alive 10+ years?
5. Benefit-cost evidence: verify the widely cited benefit-cost ratios for
   early warning (WMO, Global Commission on Adaptation) rather than repeating
   them — what do the underlying studies actually measure?
6. Does the post-disaster window historically produce durable reform in Nepal
   or performative agreements? What concretely changed after the 2024
   Kathmandu floods and the July 2025 Rasuwa flood?

OUTPUT: an institutional map with the August 2026 failure points marked; a
funding inventory table; a "why systems die" synthesis with specific design
countermeasures. Name every source in full (organization + title + date).
Where responsibility is contested or unclear, say so rather than assigning it.
```

## Prompt 6 — The hazard map: what else hangs above this corridor, and where are its cousins

```
You are researching the hazard inventory above Nepal's Bhote Koshi-Trishuli
corridor and identifying comparable high-exposure Himalayan corridors, to
establish where warning capability matters most.

CONTEXT: The 26 August 2026 disaster came from a glacier collapse near
Langtang Lirung that dammed and then released the Lhende Khola. The same
valley had a glacial outburst flood in July 2025. Reports in the days after
26 August said a new barrier lake had formed at the disaster site and was
rising. Reported exposure downstream included roughly 12 operating hydropower
plants (~431 MW) damaged and ~15 under-construction projects (~470 MW)
affected, plus bridges, roads, and a large trekking-tourism population —
verify these figures.

QUESTIONS:
1. Current status of the post-collapse barrier lake and how authorities are
   handling it (controlled drainage, monitoring, evacuation zones).
2. Hazard inventory above the corridor: which glaciers, glacial lakes, and
   unstable slopes threaten the Lhende/Bhote Koshi/Trishuli system? Use
   ICIMOD's potentially-dangerous-glacial-lake inventories, DHM/academic
   hazard assessments of the Langtang region, and any post-July-2025
   reassessments. Had any published assessment flagged this specific valley
   before 26 August 2026?
3. Exposure below: settlements and population in the flood path, the full
   hydropower fleet (operating and planned), road corridors, the dry port,
   and tourist volumes. How much new hydropower is planned in this same
   corridor despite two floods in 13 months?
4. Climate trend: what does peer-reviewed work (ICIMOD's HKH assessments,
   recent glaciology) say about ice loss acceleration, permafrost
   degradation, and expected frequency of ice-rock avalanches and GLOFs in
   the central Himalaya?
5. Generalization: which other Himalayan corridors (Nepal, Indian Himalaya,
   Bhutan, Pakistan, Tibet) combine comparable source hazards with comparable
   downstream exposure? Produce a ranked shortlist of "the next Bhote Koshis"
   with the reasoning visible — e.g., other border rivers with hydropower
   cascades and trekking populations below known unstable ice.
6. Who is monitoring what in this corridor right now, post-disaster — any
   new instruments, satellite tasking, or announced programs?

OUTPUT: a hazard-source inventory with confidence levels (measured vs modeled
vs speculative); an exposure summary; the ranked list of analogous corridors.
Name every source in full (organization + title + date). Distinguish measured
data from modeled estimates, and flag inventory gaps explicitly.
```

## Prompt 7 — What a small independent team could actually build

```
You are researching what a small, technically capable, non-governmental team
(1-3 people, software-strong, modest budget, no authority over state sensors)
could build that would add real warning value for Nepal's Bhote Koshi-Trishuli
corridor without duplicating or undermining official systems.

CONTEXT: In the 26 August 2026 disaster, detection failed (no source
monitoring; downstream gauges destroyed before alerting) but dissemination
partly worked once a human telephoned the Flood Forecasting Division: mass
SMS ~35 minutes after the event helped downstream communities. The seismic
signal of the collapse (reported M4.4-5.2) was picked up by global networks
in near-real-time but was initially misread as an earthquake. Tourists on
foreign SIMs and trekking lodges are poorly reached by national SMS.

QUESTIONS:
1. Open data inventory, with actual latencies and access mechanics: global
   and regional real-time seismic feeds (USGS, IRIS/EarthScope, and any
   networks covering the Nepal-Tibet border zone); satellite data (Sentinel-1/2
   access latency, Landsat, commercial tasking costs); Nepal DHM's public
   river-gauge data and any APIs; weather/temperature-anomaly products
   relevant to ice instability.
2. Precedents: independent, academic, or hobbyist monitoring efforts that
   became operationally useful to authorities (community seismic networks
   like Raspberry Shake, volcano-monitoring collaborations, OpenStreetMap
   crisis mapping, flood-mapping volunteers) — and cautionary cases where
   unofficial alerts caused harm, false alarms, or were ignored.
3. Candidate builds — assess each for added value, feasibility, and risk:
   (a) an automated watcher on public seismic feeds that flags
   avalanche-like signals in defined Himalayan border zones and notifies
   defined institutional recipients within minutes;
   (b) a satellite-change watchlist over named hazard sources (from ICIMOD
   inventories) with automated diff alerts;
   (c) an English-language alert relay for trekking lodges and tour
   operators that republishes official DHM/NDRRMA warnings via channels
   tourists actually receive;
   (d) an open corridor hazard dashboard consolidating gauges, alerts, and
   hazard inventories;
   (e) last-mile phone-tree/roster software for community EWS committees.
4. The ethics and liability of non-state warning: legal exposure for false
   alarms or missed events, Nepal's rules on who may issue warnings, and the
   right operating posture (feed and amplify official systems rather than
   bypass them?). What do WMO/UNDRR guidance documents say about
   non-state actors in warning chains?
5. Who to talk to before building anything: named teams at ICIMOD, DHM,
   NDRRMA, Practical Action, and university groups currently active on this
   corridor.

OUTPUT: a shortlist of the 2-3 highest-value builds, each with a one-paragraph
concept, required data sources, rough effort estimate, main failure risk, and
explicit kill criteria; then a single recommendation with reasoning. Name
every source in full (organization + title + date). Be blunt about which
candidate builds would be redundant, unwanted, or dangerous.
```
