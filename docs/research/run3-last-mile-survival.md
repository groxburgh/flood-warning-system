# Warning Minutes as Currency: Reception, Comprehension, Movement, and Survival in the Bhote Koshi–Trishuli Corridor

**Deep-research run — companion to Runs 1 and 2. Prepared 28 August 2026, two days into an active disaster. Figures below are snapshots; official tallies were still moving at time of writing and are timestamped accordingly.**

## Note on method and evidence grading

This run independently re-verified the Run 1 timeline and casualty figures against primary Nepali sources rather than accepting them as given, and found close agreement — the 08:37 NPT collapse, the 679,295-message SMS blast at 09:16, and the district death toll (538 dead as of 15:00 NPT 28 Aug) all reconfirm cleanly against named, dated sources below. Where this run's own sources diverge from or add nuance to the Run 1/Run 2 framing (especially on the central question), that is flagged explicitly. Evidence is graded throughout as **measured** (recorded in this actual event), **operationally demonstrated** (working system, this or a comparable event), **modeled** (calculated from terrain/behavioral assumptions, no ground survey), **vendor/self-reported** (NGO or agency claim, not independently checked), or **speculative**. Three searches returned nothing (marked "not found despite search") rather than being silently omitted.

## The central question: does the Chitwan/Nawalparasi toll actually show that lead time didn't help?

Run 1 flagged this as the pivotal question. Independent re-verification via NDRRMA's 15:00 NPT 28 August update (reported by **Asian News International, "Death toll reaches 538, 977 still missing as rescue teams race against fresh flood threat in Nepal," 28 August 2026**) confirms the district breakdown exactly: Chitwan 221, Nawalparasi East 134, Gorkha 46, Nuwakot 37, Dhading 33, Tanahun 28, Nawalparasi West 27, Rasuwa 12 (sums to 538). Chitwan and Nawalparasi East alone — districts that had roughly six to seven hours of warning by the Run 1/Run 2 timeline — account for 66% of confirmed deaths. Rasuwa, which had effectively none, accounts for 2.2%.

Before this is read as "comprehension is the binding constraint, not lead time," three confounds have to be named, and current public data cannot cleanly separate them:

1. **Body transport.** The **Kathmandu Post, "Nepal struggles to store and identify bodies carried downstream by devastating flood," 28 August 2026**, reports 137 of Chitwan's bodies recovered along a stretch "from Phishling to Golaghat," and states plainly that remains originating in Rasuwa and Nuwakot were recovered as far downstream as Chitwan, Tanahun and Nawalparasi — and that morgue overflow is now causing bodies to be *further* relocated between districts for storage, decoupling recovery location from both death location and origin. Authorities are tagging unidentified remains for DNA-based origin matching over the coming one to two weeks; that dataset does not exist yet.
2. **Exposed-population denominator.** Chitwan is a populous Tarai district with dense settlement directly on the Narayani/Trishuli floodplain around Bharatpur; Rasuwa's exposed riverside population is a small fraction of a sparsely populated mountain district (Uttargaya rural municipality, which contains Syabrubesi and Timure, had a total 2021 census population of 8,555 — **Nepal National Population and Housing Census 2021**). A raw death count without an exposure-population denominator cannot distinguish "warning failed" from "far more people live in the flood's path downstream."
3. **Genuine comprehension/action failure**, for which independent survey evidence (below) is strong in general but not yet available for this specific event.

**This run's assessment:** the naive reading is suggestive, not proven. What is defensible from the evidence: (a) at the two settlements essentially at the border (Timure, Syabrubesi), no warning chain — however good — could have helped, because the flood arrived before Nepal's Flood Forecasting Division even learned the event had occurred; (b) at settlements with 20–90 minutes of lead time (Betrawati, Bidur), outcomes split sharply between institutionally pre-organized groups (the Bidur school, below) and everyone else; (c) downstream of roughly Galchhi, lead time was abundant (70 minutes to 7 hours) and the constraint mechanically shifts to comprehension, trust, and decision — which is exactly where the peer-reviewed survey evidence says Nepal's system is weakest. The marginal value of additional lead time appears to collapse once a population already has a rehearsed, low-decision-cost response; it stays high for everyone else. Resolving the body-transport/denominator confound requires the origin-tracing dataset NDRRMA says is weeks away — treat any single-number claim about "where people actually died" as unknown until then.

Population figures are split between confirmed census totals (municipality-wide, not settlement-core) and modeled settlement estimates, because — a second data gap this run turned up — no ward-level exposure population exists either.

Two structural points follow. First, at Timure and Syabrubesi, *no last-mile intervention discussed in this report could have moved the outcome* — the physical wave-transit time from the Tibet-side trigger to these settlements was shorter than any human-relay warning chain could operate, a genuine hard floor, not a policy failure. Second, from Galchhi downstream, the physical "can people walk to high ground in time" question is essentially solved by lead time alone (modeled >95% reach safety within 15 minutes of decision) — meaning every dollar spent on lead time beyond that point buys progressively less, and every dollar spent on comprehension and trust buys progressively more. **Lead-time investment and comprehension investment have different marginal returns at different points on this corridor, and the crossover is roughly at Betrawati/Bidur.**

## 2. Warning-to-action latency: which terms dominate

The full chain: **impact time − (detection + classification + decision + transmission + comprehension + movement)**. This event's measured values, reconfirmed via **New Spotlight Nepal, "Bhotekoshi Flood: The Warning That Came by Telephone," 28 August 2026** (citing the Flood Forecasting Division's own operational log):

- Detection (by telephone, not instrument): 08:37 collapse → 09:00 FFD received calls — **23 minutes**, human-relay, not automated. Run 2's modeled 90–130 second automated-detection system is roughly **10–15x faster** than what actually operated.
- Classification/confirmation: 09:00 → 09:05, **5 minutes**.
- Decision/transmission: 09:05 → first public warning 09:15, SMS dispatch 09:16 — **10–11 minutes**.
- Comprehension + reaction: **Budimir et al., "Communicating complex forecasts: an analysis of the approach in Nepal's flood early warning system," Geoscience Communication (EGU), 2020** — a mixed-methods study (11 national key-informant interviews, a 25-person workshop, 7 district interviews in Bardiya, a 127-respondent community survey) found: only **33% of community respondents had ever received an SMS warning** at all; of those who had, **40% found the message "very hard to understand"** and 57% rated their own comprehension at 5/10 or lower; **46% said they were "very unlikely to act"** versus 21% "very likely"; **56% described acting on a warning as "very hard,"** with illiteracy (27%) and difficulty making decisions (16%) the leading barriers; comprehension and willingness both significantly worse among women, older adults, and Dalit respondents; and **61% preferred in-person warning, 17% preferred voice message** — meaning SMS, the system's actual primary channel, ranks behind both preferred formats. The paper quotes a DHM forecaster on why uncertainty is not communicated: *"Communicate forecast uncertainty only if you can convince; then otherwise it is better not to communicate as it will lead to a false message."* DHM's forecasting team, per this paper, was four people in 2017; current 2026 staffing was not verified this session.
- Movement: modeled per Section 1, essentially unconstrained (physically achievable) from Galchhi downstream given observed lead times.

**Which terms dominate:** upstream of Betrawati, detection-to-transmission latency dominates and is close to a hard floor set by physics. Downstream of Galchhi, comprehension and willingness-to-act dominate overwhelmingly — the Budimir figures suggest that even a hypothetical instantaneous, perfectly worded warning would still see roughly half the recipient population fail to act on it. No event-specific post-2026 comprehension survey exists yet; the Budimir figures are the best available baseline, not this event's measured outcome.

## 3. Channels: what exists, what doesn't, and what each wall costs to fix

**What was actually used on 26 August** (Spotlight Nepal): 679,295 SMS via Nepal Telecom and Ncell starting 09:16 with updates at 10:28, 11:50 and 13:00; 147 telephone calls; 30 media interviews; 11 Facebook posts; 2 live broadcasts. No siren, no cell broadcast.

**Cell broadcast / CAP.** Nepal has not deployed operational cell broadcast. Per **PreventionWeb (UNDRR), "Early Warnings for All Initiative (EW4All): January 2026 Updates,"** Nepal's national EW4All Roadmap 2026–2030 was still in development, with a cell-broadcast *readiness validation* workshop (ITU with GSMA) held 11 November 2025 — nine and a half months before this disaster — and a roadmap dissemination workshop tentatively planned for February 2026. Cell broadcast is a one-to-many geofenced broadcast that does not depend on a subscriber list or network queuing and would reach any handset camped on a targeted tower, including foreign roaming SIMs (general GSM/3GPP architecture, not a Nepal-specific source). **The wall:** regulatory framework, a national CAP aggregator, operator-side enablement — plausibly multi-year on the current roadmap. **Interim workaround:** bulk SMS to known subscribers, structurally unable to guarantee foreign-SIM reach (unconfirmed either way whether roaming numbers were in the 679,295). **Buildable regardless:** a mandatory SMS opt-in captured at the point tourists already pass a government checkpoint — TIMS or park-entry registration in Rasuwa — closing part of the foreign-tourist gap with no telecom-side change at all.

**Sirens — the Tsho Rolpa case as the load-bearing precedent.** Per **Kathmandu Post (20 May 2015; 10 December 2015)**, a two-sensor Community-Based Early Warning System was installed at Tsho Rolpa in 1998 with World Bank funding; it **ceased functioning in 2002** (poor management compounded by the insurgency period); DHM installed a new automated pilot in 2015 with UNDP and Practical Action. Per **Dialogue Earth, "Nepal Worries About Its Most Dangerous Glacial Lake"** (site visits 2021 and 2023), the system is **not functioning today**: Gaurishankar rural municipality chairperson Nima Geli Sherpa — *"The early warning systems were damaged long ago, and we don't even have a good mobile phone network in the area"* — and no government staff observed at the lake on either visit. A documented, on-the-record, two-cycle failure over 25 years at Nepal's most closely watched glacial lake. **The wall is not technological** — hardware installed on a project grant decays without a permanently funded local staffing line. **Cost to break it:** convert siren maintenance to a recurring government budget item with a named responsible office. **Buildable regardless:** a mandatory annual pre-monsoon independent audit of any siren system — cheap, immediate, converts *silent* decay into a *known* gap.

**FM/community radio, loudspeakers, Facebook.** Nepal has an extensive community radio sector used in disaster response (UNESCO's page on Nepali community radios blocked automated fetch; reach/latency figures **not verified this session**). Radio's structural advantage: a voice channel, closer to the 61%/17% in-person/voice preference in the Budimir survey than SMS. Facebook and the 30 media interviews/2 live broadcasts plausibly reach urban, literate, connected populations and may generate a secondary relay effect (urban relatives phoning exposed rural households) — a mechanism this research could not confirm or rule out. Loudspeakers and trained local task forces are explicitly a **recommendation, not existing infrastructure** (PreventionWeb expert interview).

## 4. Special populations

**The Bidur school — what specifically worked.** Tribhuvan Trishuli Secondary School in Bidur, 1,650 enrolled students; per both **Spotlight Nepal** and **Nepalnews** (both citing principal Rajendra Dawadi by name), the alert arrived during morning assembly and every child on the premises was moved to high ground. Nepalnews includes the essential caveat from the principal: *"We have no way of knowing the status of those who were walking to school on the roads."* What made it work is identifiable and transferable: (1) the population was already physically consolidated, removing the search-and-gather step; (2) an existing chain of command (teachers) could execute a pre-understood action without each individual independently comprehending and deciding — precisely the step the Budimir survey identifies as the most common failure point; (3) roughly 30–60 minutes of modeled lead time was enough for organized group movement, though the same window is not enough for the 46% of individual households "very unlikely to act." **This is an institutional-drill effect, not a warning-technology effect** — the same SMS that saved this school reached individual households with far less reliable results.

**Hydropower tunnel workers.** Per **Radio Nepal** and **OnlineKhabar** (28 August 2026), 350 workers were extracted alive from the Upper Trishuli tunnel, with 100 more still being rescued as of those reports. **Neither source, nor any other found, states how workers were warned or why some survived** — a genuine, specific data gap despite dedicated searching. A generic hydropower disaster-management-plan template from a *different* Nepali developer (Nepal Water and Energy Development Co. disclosure document) requires "provision of evacuation paths, setting up of alarms and warning systems" as boilerplate licensing language — but this is **not confirmed to be Upper Trishuli-1's own plan**. Whether Upper Trishuli-1's plan was audited, current, or executed is **unknown**.

**Trekking lodges and tour operators.** No organized, institution-wide warning chain to lodges or guides was found in any source searched. The Timure customs chief's account (via a trekking-company blog, lower reliability, used only for the direct quote) — *"we saw the flood coming down from above like a volcanic eruption."* Whether foreign-SIM tourists downstream received the SMS blast is **unconfirmed either way**. Unaccounted-tourist figures moved fast and are inconsistent across snapshots: 403 unaccounted per Nepal Tourism Board CEO (CBS News, 27 Aug), 596 foreign nationals from 33 countries missing as of 20:00 NPT 27 Aug (Nepal Ministry of Foreign Affairs updated statement), 517 as of 28 Aug (ANI/NDRRMA; Kathmandu Post) — treat all as timestamped snapshots.

## 5. Multi-pulse hazard: who warns the rescuers

A barrier lake formed at the Chhochen Khola/Purepu Tsangpo confluence in Tibet and partially breached 27–28 August. Per **The Week** and **Al Jazeera** (28 August 2026), China notified Nepal of the breach; Rasuwa CDO Narendra Pariyar is quoted — *"we can see that the level of water in the river is rising"* — an explicitly **visual, ad hoc observation, not an instrumented trigger** — after which Nepal paused search operations roughly 90 minutes and Chinese teams pulled back independently. **No source describes a formal, pre-established protocol** for warning responders of secondary hazards; the two countries' pauses are reported as parallel, not jointly coordinated. **The wall:** instrumenting the barrier lake requires siting sensors in Tibet. **Interim workaround:** what happened — a district official's visual judgment, functional this once, neither fast nor scalable. **Buildable regardless of the transboundary wall:** a standing procedural rule requiring any responder team operating downstream of a known fresh barrier lake to work on fixed radio check-in intervals with a predetermined automatic-withdrawal trigger — an institutional fix needing no new instrument and no Chinese cooperation.

## 6. Community-based EWS: the Karnali gauge-reader model

Evaluated in **Khanal, Gautam et al., "Community-based early warning systems for flood risk mitigation in Nepal," Natural Hazards and Earth System Sciences, 2017** (peer-reviewed — the highest-grade source in this section, distinct from Practical Action's own self-published cost-benefit summary): trained community "watchpersons," mostly part-time and locally embedded, relay river-stage readings by mobile phone/SMS downstream under a predefined protocol. **Lead times:** 2–3 hours from the current CBEWS, extendable to 7–8 hours with hydrological forecasting; a practitioner report (**CTC-N, "Flood Early Warning System in Practice: Experiences of Nepal"**) puts the Chisapani-gauge system at 1.5–4.5 hours depending on community distance. **Performance in real floods was mixed:** it "worked effectively" in the Karnali and West Rapti basins during the 2014 monsoon, but **failed in the adjacent Babai basin the same season because the hydro-met station itself was washed away** — a single-point-of-failure mode directly analogous to what happened to Syabrubesi's own station in 2026. **Sustainability:** the NHESS paper documents a named structural defect — during an actual flood a watchperson "needed to take care of their own family and properties," which "sometimes resulted in inadequate warning to the communities." **Cost figures could not be obtained**: three separate documents attempted; none contained capex, opex, or a benefit-cost ratio; Practical Action's underlying cost-benefit paper inaccessible this session. **A genuine, specific evidence gap**, not filled with an invented number.

## 7. The location-based instruction concept — assessment

No operational system found anywhere sends a recipient a personalized instruction of the form "go to [specific named safe point], via [specific route], from your current location," for an open-terrain natural-hazard evacuation at population scale. What exists:

- **Polygon-targeted alerts** (cell broadcast, WEA) narrow *who* receives a message but deliver identical generic text — not personalized routing. Nepal does not yet operate this at all.
- **Google Flood Hub** (India) provides "a map, expected timeline, as well as tips" — risk information, not routing, not deployed in Nepal.
- **Nepal's BIPAD portal** (UNDRR case study, drawing on the government's own account — institutional self-report) overlays exposure and household-risk data for **decision-makers**; a planning dashboard, "being tested," no independent evaluation found, no confirmed link to individual-facing push alerts.
- Genuinely personalized route-level guidance exists in the academic literature only for **indoor, micro-scale contexts** (building fire evacuation via smartphone indoor positioning) — a fundamentally different problem from open rural mountain terrain with sparse coverage and, per Run 2's finding reconfirmed here, no surveyed safe-point dataset to personalize *against*.

**Verdict: not operationally demonstrated anywhere found for this use case; research-stage at best.** Its prerequisite — a village-by-village safe-point and route survey — does not exist for any settlement in this corridor. **Cost to break it:** the route survey itself (labor-intensive but low-tech, plausibly days per settlement) plus a static, offline-cacheable "nearest safe point from ward X" lookup distributable over SMS/USSD. **Interim workaround:** the physically robust general rule needing no survey — get elevation, get lateral distance from the channel — over whatever channel exists. **Buildable regardless:** the survey has independent value for drills, signage, and Section 1's table even before any smart-alerting layer.
