# Institutional Design Constraints for a Himalayan Cascading-Hazard Early Warning System: Who Detects, Decides, Warns, and Evacuates in Nepal — De Jure vs De Facto

**Research run 3 of a companion pack on the 26 August 2026 Bhote Koshi disaster. Prepared 28 August 2026, two days after the event, while a new barrier lake is actively forming at the same site.**

## Framing

This run maps the institutional skeleton onto the failure sequence documented by companion Run 1. The central finding, stated up front because it cuts against the intuitive "political cowardice" framing: **once Nepal's Department of Hydrology and Meteorology (DHM) had a signal, it warned fast and was not legally exposed for doing so.** The catastrophic delay on 26 August 2026 sat almost entirely *upstream* of the decision-to-warn node — in detection and cross-border transmission — not in an official's reluctance to pull a trigger. That reframes where design effort should go: less "how do we get someone to act bravely" and more "how do we get a true signal to a legally empowered actor before the water arrives." Where political and legal friction *does* bind — pre-event investment, non-state alerting, unfunded devolved mandates — this report treats each as a cost to be worked around, not a verdict to stop at.

## 1. Failure-Chain Diagram, 26 August 2026

Sources triangulated below: Run 1 finding (companion, treated as input); Nepalnews (English), *"Bhote Koshi catastrophe exposes limits of Nepal's flood warning system,"* long read, 27–28 Aug 2026; NESRA Space, *"NESRA FloodWatch: Rasuwa–Bhotekoshi Flood 2026"*; Kathmandu Post, *"Bhotekoshi flood: What we know, what remains unclear,"* 26 Aug 2026; Amarnepal, *"What Caused the Bhote Koshi Flood,"* 2026; Ratopati English, *"Authorities Instruct Evacuation After Rasuwa Flood,"* 26 Aug 2026.

| # | Node | Status | Evidence |
|---|------|--------|----------|
| 1 | Signal generated | WORKED (physically) / FAILED (institutionally) | An ice-rock mass detached at ~5,200 m on the Tibetan side, ~20 km NE of the border, fell ~1,200 m into the Lhende Khola, dammed and then breached — a real, physically detectable event (USGS confirmed the seismic signature was collapse, not earthquake, per severe-weather.eu/NESRA reporting). The signal existed; no sensor of any kind sat in that catchment. |
| 2 | Signal observed | FAILED | Nothing observed it at origin. The Lhende Khola tributary is, in the Nepalnews long read's words, a catchment "Nepal does not monitor." No confirmation China observed and shared it either — the Chinese Embassy's public position (via The Statesman, 27 Aug 2026) attributed the suddenness to the collapse itself, not to a withheld observation. |

Transmission was ultimately accomplished by humans on the ground with phones — not by any engineered channel. |

The two accounts diverge by 5–10 minutes on exact timestamps but agree on the mechanism: **a human phone chain, not automated interpretation of gauge data**, because the gauges were about to be destroyed, not tripped. |
| 5 | Authority notified | WORKED (fast, once triggered) | DHM's Flood Forecasting Division (FFD) was notified by ~09:00 per Run 1, ~08:56 per Nepalnews. NDRRMA acted as relay, not as an independent detector — the Nepalnews long read states explicitly NDRRMA had "no direct border monitoring capability." |
| 6 | Decision made | WORKED | FFD staff decided to issue mass SMS within minutes of notification — this was a technical/operational decision inside DHM, not one that waited for ministerial or Cabinet sign-off. No evidence of hesitation once information arrived. |

Travel advisories also issued for the Prithvi Highway corridor. |
| 8 | Received | PARTIAL | Reception was uneven. A school (Tribhuvan Trishuli Secondary, Nuwakot) received the alert during morning assembly. Hundreds of trekkers, transport workers, and market-area residents directly on the river corridor — closer to the source and earlier in the flood's path than the SMS blast could reach — did not receive any warning before the water arrived; 403 people were reported missing (341 foreign, 62 Nepali) in the first-day count (Nepalnews explainer, 26 Aug 2026). |
| 9 | Understood | WORKED / FAILED, bimodal | Where received, understanding was generally not the bottleneck (school evacuation succeeded fully — 1,650 students moved to high ground). Where the chain failed at nodes 1–4 (upstream travelers, Rasuwagadhi/Timure/Syabrubesi residents nearest the border), no action was possible in the available minutes. Security forces (5,000+ Nepali Army personnel, 13 helicopters) mobilized within hours for search and rescue — but that is post-event response, a separate node from pre-event action. |

**Read of the chain**: the failure clusters entirely in nodes 1–4 (generation through interpretation) — the part of the chain with zero instrumentation in the actual source zone and reliant on humans discovering a fait accompli. Nodes 5–7 (notify, decide, send) worked in under 20–25 minutes by any account. Node 8 (received) and node 10 (action) split cleanly by geography: whoever was far enough downstream to be inside the SMS/highway-advisory shadow, and awake/attentive enough to receive it, mostly survived; whoever was between the border and the first working alert never had a chance. This matches Run 1's automated-network finding: the first automated threshold crossing was at 11:20, roughly 100 km downstream — two hours after people started dying near the border.

## 2. De Jure vs De Facto Authority

Primary source: Government of Nepal, **Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Act, 2074 (2017)**, English translation hosted by the District Administration Office, Rasuwa (Ministry of Home Affairs). Cross-checked against World Bank, *Implementation Completion and Results Report — Nepal: Building Resilience to Climate-Related Hazards Project* (ICR00005440); ODI (Overseas Development Institute), Emily Wilkinson and Emma Gogerty et al., *"The governance of Nepal's flood early warning system: opportunities under federalism,"* BRACED working paper, 27 Aug 2019.

### 2.1 What the law assigns

| Actor | De jure role (statutory basis) | De facto role, 26 Aug 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| **DHM** | **Not named in the DRRM Act at all.** DHM has, per the World Bank's 2021 project completion report, no dedicated founding act — it exists as a Department under the Ministry of Energy, Water Resources and Irrigation by administrative elevation (1962 → 1988), not statute. Its hazard-monitoring role is implied only indirectly, through NDRRMA's Section 11(p)/(q) powers to "conduct study... on hydrology and meteorology" and run the national Emergency Operations Centre. | Was the actual technical detector, decision-maker, and disseminator — issued the SMS blast, ran the Flood Forecasting Division, absorbed 147 phone calls/30 media interviews/11 Facebook posts/2 live broadcasts the same day (Run 1). Functioned as the de facto national warning authority despite having no statutory warning mandate of its own. |
| **NDRRMA** | Created by the Act as "central resource agency" (§11b); operates the National Emergency Operations Centre "to provide early information about likely occurrence of disasters" (§11q); coordinates search/rescue and relief (§11f). | Relay node between the district and DHM, plus public-facing spokesperson function (Shanti Mahat). Nepalnews reports NDRRMA had "no direct border monitoring capability" of its own — the Act's §11q early-information function was performed, in practice, by DHM and district staff on the phone. |
| **CDO / District Disaster Management Committee** | Chaired by the CDO (§16(1)). Empowered to run search/rescue, distribute relief, control movement in affected areas (§16(2)d–f) — but on the question of declaring a disaster, the Committee may only **"recommend to Government of Nepal"** (§16(2)t). It cannot declare on its own authority. | Rasuwa's district administration was the first body to detect and phone in the event (~08:56–09:00). Once the disaster was visible, CDOs nationwide received direct relocation instructions — reported by Ratopati English (26 Aug 2026) as coming from the Prime Minister personally, not routed through the Committee's nominal recommend-then-wait channel. |
| **Government of Nepal (Cabinet)** | Sole authority to declare a "disaster threatening area" by Gazette notification (§32(1)); sole authority to requisition property and restrict movement in a declared area (§33(1)). | Declared Rasuwa, Nuwakot, and Dhading "disaster crisis zones" for three months under §32, same day (New Spotlight Magazine, 26 Aug 2026; confirmed independently by Ratopati English, Khabarhub, and Republica). This is the one node where the de jure and de facto chains matched cleanly — but it fired *after* the SMS warnings, as a relief/legal-status instrument, not as the life-saving trigger. |
| **Local government / municipality** | Since the 2017 federal restructuring and the **Local Government Operation Act, 2074**, municipalities must run Local Emergency Operations Centres, maintain local disaster plans, and — per the post-federalization ODI/BRACED analysis — are now expected to pay for and maintain local gauge readers and hydro-met stations, with "no clear federal funding mechanism" identified for that transfer. | The school principal at Tribhuvan Trishuli Secondary made an individual, front-line evacuation call for 1,650 students on receiving the alert — the kind of last-mile judgment the Act formally assigns to local disaster committees, exercised here as an ad hoc institutional actor rather than a resourced LEOC function. |
| **Security forces (Army, Police, APF)** | Not primary detectors or alert-issuers under the Act; mobilized under the Council/Executive Committee's disaster-response coordination powers. | De facto operational lead once the disaster was underway: 5,000+ Army personnel, 13 helicopters, five hospitals on standby. Search-and-rescue and relief, not detection or warning. |
| **Hydropower licensees** | **No general statutory EWS obligation was located** in Nepal's Electricity Act/licensing framework itself. Where EWS obligations exist, they are project-specific safeguard conditions attached by international financiers — confirmed for Upper Trishuli-1 (NWEDC), whose ADB/AIIB-conditioned Disaster Management Plan mandated radar-level sensors and audio alerting specifically at **Mailung Bazar and Betrawati** — the same Betrawati location named as a destroyed DHM gauge site in Run 1. | Whether the Upper Trishuli-1 project's own Betrawati/Mailung Bazar EWS fired, failed, or was itself destroyed on 26 August is **UNKNOWN** — not established in any source reviewed. Given the project was 84% complete and lost over 90% of its upper dam and base camp (Korea JoongAng Daily, 27 Aug 2026), it is plausible the EWS infrastructure itself was in the flood's direct path. This is a specific, answerable open question for follow-up. |

**Contested**: Whether hydropower licensees carry any *general, domestic-law* obligation (as opposed to project-specific IFI conditions) to fund or maintain downstream warning systems is contested — not resolved by any single statute located in this research. This matters directly for the corridor: at least 12 operating and 15 under-construction hydropower projects sit on the Bhote Koshi/Trishuli system (New Spotlight, 26 Aug 2026), each a potential co-funder of exactly the sensor network that failed.

**Cost of the wall, workaround, and buildable path**: Establishing a universal domestic EWS obligation would require amending the Electricity Act 2049 or Nepal Electricity Regulatory Commission licensing rules — a multi-year legislative process contingent on parliamentary bandwidth NDRRMA does not control. The interim workaround is contractual, not statutory: NEA, IFI co-financiers (ADB, AIIB, World Bank), and provincial governments can write EWS-plus-O&M-funding clauses into *new* power purchase agreements and refinancing terms immediately, project by project, without waiting on Parliament — precisely as ADB/AIIB already did for Upper Trishuli-1. What's buildable right now, within the existing wall: a voluntary consortium of the corridor's dozen-plus operating licensees co-funding a shared sensor network upstream of all their intakes, modeled on El Salvador's Rio Lempa public-private system (see Section 4) — each licensee has direct financial exposure to exactly this kind of loss, which is the same incentive structure that has kept the El Salvador system running for 17+ years.

Similarly, DHM's total absence from the DRRM Act's text is a durable structural gap, not a one-off oversight: the World Bank's 2021 completion report notes a **Hydromet Bill** was drafted specifically to give DHM a dedicated legal foundation and fee-based cost-recovery mechanism (via aviation weather services) — and was still pending in Parliament at that project's 2020 close. No source reviewed here shows it has since passed. That is six-plus years of a known institutional fix sitting unenacted. The interim workaround already in use is exactly what happened on 26 August: DHM operates on delegated, executive-level authority under NDRRMA's Section 11 powers and the Ministry of Energy's administrative structure — functional but legally thin, meaning DHM's mandate, budget, and independence remain fully subject to executive and fiscal discretion rather than a statutory floor. What's buildable without the Bill: NDRRMA could issue implementing regulations under the existing DRRM Rules, 2076 (2019) that explicitly name DHM as the technical authority for hazard monitoring — closing the gap administratively without needing primary legislation.

## 3. The Legal Right to Warn: A Binding Design Constraint

Primary sources: DRRM Act 2074 text (as above); Kathmandu Post, *"Nepal's electronic transactions law continues to be misused to stifle free speech,"* 29 Apr 2026 (analysis of the **Electronic Transactions Act, 2006 (2063)**).

**No explicit monopoly found.** The DRRM Act does not contain language reserving hazard-warning issuance exclusively to NDRRMA or DHM. What it contains instead is a **criminal backstop against false alarms, applicable to "whoever"** — not limited to state actors:

> **Section 24(2)**: "Whoever makes or circulates a false alarm or warning as to disaster or its severity or magnitude leading to panic" — fine up to Rs 100,000, or imprisonment up to six months, or both.

Critically, this sits next to **Section 31**, a good-faith immunity clause: *"No suit or prosecution... shall lie in any court against any civil servant or employee... for any work done or intended to be done in good faith under the provisions of this Act."* That protection is written for state employees. No equivalent explicit good-faith shield for a non-state actor was located in the Act.

Layered on top: the **Electronic Transactions Act 2006, Section 47** — up to five years' imprisonment and a Rs 100,000 fine for electronic content deemed "against public decency," to "spread hatred," or to "disrupt harmony," terms the Kathmandu Post's analysis calls "unclear, ambiguous and contradictory" (citing a 2024 Office of the Attorney General study using the same language). Documented use pattern in Kathmandu District Court, 2013–2023: 70 expression-related cases, 64% immediate arrest, average 38-day detention, 60% conviction rate — yet in **fewer than 10% of cases did the court find actual harm occurred**. This is a broad, vague, demonstrably-used-against-speech statute that a public-facing independent alert (especially via app or social media) would sit inside.

**As a design constraint, this is asymmetric and directional, not a flat prohibition:**

- **Alerting institutions** (feeding DHM, NDRRMA, a District or Local Emergency Operations Centre with a signal — e.g., a dead-man/station-loss alarm, a model output, a dashboard) is comparatively low-risk. It is not "a warning to the public leading to panic" under Section 24(2)'s plain language, and it fits inside the Act's existing architecture of information flowing toward the bodies legally empowered to declare and warn.
- **Alerting the public directly** (SMS/app push to residents) is not explicitly illegal if true and issued in good faith — but it is legally exposed in a way government issuance is not, because Section 31's immunity does not, on the text located, extend to non-officials, and Section 47's vague standard has a demonstrated prosecution pattern against digital speech generally.

**Cost / workaround / buildable, stated plainly**: Changing this wall requires either a statutory amendment adding an explicit good-faith defense for any actor issuing a hazard warning based on credible data (amending Section 24(2) or adding a proviso), or a formal accreditation regime under NDRRMA rules recognizing approved third-party disseminators — neither exists today in any source reviewed. The interim workaround is institutional, not legal: seek a formal MOU or delegated authorization from NDRRMA/DHM/a municipality *before* operating a public-facing channel, so the non-state system is acting "for" or "under" a public authority and can plausibly borrow Section 31's shield by proxy; keep an auditable data/decision trail to support a good-faith defense if ever prosecuted; and use attributive, hedged public language ("per DHM's published threshold..." rather than independent claims of fact). What remains fully buildable *right now*, with no legal exposure at all under this reading: any B2G (business/NGO-to-government) product — the station-loss "dead-man" alarm Run 1 costed out, model dashboards, satellite change-detection feeds — routed to DHM/NDRRMA/District EOC dashboards rather than to the public. This is also the higher-leverage build: it directly targets the actual failed nodes (1–4) rather than duplicating node 7, which already worked.

**Not addressed here**: no Nepali court case testing this exact question (a non-state hazard warning prosecuted, or defended, under Section 24(2) or ETA Section 47) was located in the time available for this run; treat the legal exposure as a plausible reading of the statutory text and documented prosecution pattern, not as a confirmed precedent.

## 4. Trigger Political Economy — A Correction to the Expected Framing

General literature: Allison G. S. Knox, *"The Politics of False Alarms and Evacuation Decisions,"* American Military University (AMU Edge); UNDRR/WMO, *Global Status of Multi-Hazard Early Warning Systems 2022*. Nepal-specific: Nepalnews, *"Paper plans, watery graves: Negligence in disaster preparedness."*

The general literature (Knox) frames evacuation-trigger political economy as a "cry-wolf" problem: officials under-warn because a false alarm is politically costly and erodes future compliance. **This event does not fit that model well.** Once DHM's FFD had a signal, it acted in roughly 15–20 minutes, sent warnings to over 679,000 phones, and faced no apparent internal hesitation. Nepal's Section 31 good-faith immunity plausibly reinforces this: a government official's *personal* legal exposure for a wrong-but-good-faith warning is already low, which should reduce (not increase) the incentive to sit on a signal. The bottleneck on 26 August was not an official afraid to be wrong — it was the near-total absence of anything to be right or wrong *about* until the water was already crossing the border.

Where political economy *does* bind, based on this research, is earlier and later in the cycle, not at the moment of decision:

- **Pre-event investment.** NDRRMA spokesperson Shanti Mahat, quoted in the Nepalnews "Paper plans, watery graves" piece: *"We do not have sophisticated equipment needed for search and rescue, and lack budget even for procurement."* The same piece notes Nepal's Disaster Management Fund is structured around post-disaster relief, not pre-disaster mitigation — a fiscal architecture that systematically under-prices prevention, whose payoff is an invisible non-event, against relief, whose payoff is politically visible. This is the real "who pays for something that (if it works) looks like nothing happened" problem, and it sits in budget design, not trigger design.
- **Post-event, top-down override of the formal chain.** The Prime Minister issued relocation instructions directly to CDOs (Ratopati English, 26 Aug 2026), bypassing the Act's nominal "CDO recommends, Government declares" sequence (§16(2)(t) → §32(1)) once the disaster was already visible. That sequence is built for *before* a visible disaster, functioning as intended in this case only as a legal/relief-status formality (the Section 32 zone declaration) that trailed the life-saving action by hours.

**What research on trigger design (forecast-based financing, parametric/anticipatory-action triggers — evidenced generically here, not Nepal-specifically) recommends, and how it maps**: pre-agree public, auditable thresholds *before* the crisis so no individual has to personally own an in-the-moment judgment call; pre-authorize the response plan and its funding so crossing the threshold doesn't itself require fresh political sign-off; use multiple confirmatory sources to avoid single-point false triggers. Nepal already has one instrument built exactly on these lines — the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA), *Nepal Anticipatory Action Framework – Floods (2025)* — whose detailed trigger mechanics were not extracted in this run's search budget; flagged here as the concrete existing instrument most relevant to formalizing a pre-agreed automated trigger for this corridor, and worth direct follow-up. A **dead-man/station-loss alarm** (Run 1's costed intervention) is a direct application of the same design logic at the front end of the chain: it converts an uncertain, discretionary "should I call this in" human judgment into a mechanical "silence from the gauge = alarm" rule, removing hesitation from exactly the node (interpretation/notification) that actually failed — while leaving open, and unresolved by this research, the institutional question of who owns such an alarm and who is authorized to act on its output (see Section 2).

## 6. Why Systems Die — Synthesis and Countermeasures

Sources: WMO, *"Building Hydrometeorological Early Warning Capacity in Developing Countries: Successes and Failures,"* WMO Magazine article; PrepareCenter, *"Disaster Early Warning Systems in Nepal: Institutional and Operational Frameworks"*; ODI/BRACED, Wilkinson & Gogerty et al. (2019); World Bank BRCH ICR (2021); MDPI/PMC, *"Building Resilience from the Grassroots: The Cyclone Preparedness Programme at 50,"* 2022.

**Documented failure mode**: **Tso Rolpa GLOF early warning system**, installed 1998 with downstream siren infrastructure, is confirmed **"no longer in operation"** — a specific, named Nepali decay case, not a hypothetical. The WMO magazine piece generalizes the mechanism across developing-country NMHSs: leadership turnover losing institutional advocates, budget/maintenance shortfalls, difficulty retaining trained staff, and — pointedly — "various competing and unconnected activities leading to limited use and high cost of maintenance," as donor projects layer on top of each other without integration. Nepal's own 2024 Disaster Report (DPNet-hosted) independently uses almost identical language: "response capacity building is scattered and project-based."

**A live, structural decay risk, not yet resolved**: the ODI/BRACED 2019 analysis flags that Nepal's 2017 federal restructuring pushed gauge-reader payment and station-maintenance funding down to municipalities *without* a clear matching federal funding mechanism — meaning the newest layer of Nepal's institutional design may be building the next Tso Rolpa in slow motion, by unfunded mandate rather than donor exit.

**Evidence of longer survival, and who paid**:

- **El Salvador, Rio Lempa system** (WMO magazine): established 2001 as a public-private partnership between the national meteorological service and the hydropower/energy utility CEL — "still operational today," 17+ years. Who pays: the utility, because it has direct financial exposure to the same river.
- **Bangladesh Cyclone Preparedness Programme**: 50+ years, government + Bangladesh Red Crescent Society, ~76,000 community volunteers (90%+ unpaid), organized in gender-balanced village units. Who pays: government/Red Crescent core structure; the last-mile workforce is compensated in social standing, not salary — the source notes this is a live tension even in this best-case example ("ongoing tension about whether stipends should be introduced"), not a fully solved problem. A named success factor: single-hazard focus made the model "easier to replicate district to district" than India, Pakistan, or the Philippines' more hierarchical multi-hazard equivalents.
- **Afghanistan** (WMO/USAID project): survival attributed to identifying "a technical champion" inside the institution plus a systematic modernization roadmap, rather than to funding volume alone.
- **Nepal's own BRCH project** (World Bank, 2013–2020) shows the same principle can fail *within* one project: DHM's core O&M budget was adequate for its network ($8.9M/yr against a $1.44M need at closure), but the co-located agromet/AMIS program's approved annual budget was just $26,000 — "potentially inadequate," in the World Bank's own words. Uneven funding across sub-systems inside a single donor project is itself a decay vector.

### Who-pays models, ranked by evidenced durability

| Model | Recurring-cost payer | Nepal-relevant evidence | Durability signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Donor capital grant, no dedicated O&M plan | Nobody, by default | Tso Rolpa (1998 → defunct) | Documented failure |
| State core budget line | Central government | DHM's O&M line — adequate in isolation (2020–21) but exposed to whole-of-government fiscal swings (46.79% capital execution in FY2082/83; DHM's own multi-year decline per Run 1) | Necessary, not sufficient — vulnerable to macro-fiscal and political-cycle risk |
| Unfunded devolution | Nominally municipalities, mechanism unclear | ODI/BRACED 2019 finding, post-2017 federalization |未resolved live risk, not yet a confirmed failure but flagged as one in progress |
| Private co-payer with direct stake | Utility/hydropower licensee | El Salvador (CEL, 17+ yrs); Nepal's Upper Trishuli-1 project-specific EWS at Betrawati/Mailung Bazar (status on 26 Aug unknown) | Best-evidenced durability mechanism located, but scope limited to the payer's own footprint |
| Volunteer/community, compensated in social capital | Community, subsidized by government/Red Crescent core | Bangladesh CPP, 50+ years | Longest-surviving model found, but requires single-hazard simplicity and decades of sustained institutional commitment — not a quick transplant |
| Regional donor pooling | Multi-donor fund, explicitly acknowledging the gap it doesn't close | CREWS South Asia ($5.25M/2025–29): document itself states regional NMHSs are "unable to cover operational expenses through their own budget" | Funds capacity-building, not recurring O&M — gap acknowledged, not solved |

**Countermeasures, concretely**: (1) any new sensor or alarm installed in this corridor should be co-funded and co-owned by the hydropower licensees with direct downstream exposure, on the El Salvador/Upper Trishuli-1 model, rather than installed as a pure donor capital grant; (2) any dead-man/station-loss alarm (Run 1) should be built with its O&M line — telecom SIM costs, battery replacement, a named responsible party — specified and funded *before* installation, not left implicit; (3) federal transfers matching the post-2017 devolved gauge-maintenance mandate should be made explicit rather than assumed, closing the ODI/BRACED-flagged gap; (4) a single-hazard, volunteer-based last-mile layer (CPP-style) is the best-evidenced low-cost durability mechanism for the "received/understood/acted" end of the chain specifically, distinct from the sensor/detection end where the private-co-payer model fits better.

**What the past predicts**: the same specific fix — real-time cross-border data sharing for this exact corridor — has now been announced or re-announced at least three times (2023, August 2025, May 2026) across two escalating disasters without becoming operational. That is a measured, three-year base rate, not a one-off lapse. Absent a structurally different instrument — a binding, financed, treaty-level mechanism with infrastructure on both sides, rather than another "understanding" — this research finds no basis to expect the pattern breaks on its own within the next reform cycle. The one place the record shows genuine follow-through is money that flows through project finance with IFI safeguard conditions attached (Upper Trishuli-1's EWS, ADB's Terai FFEWS) — suggesting that conditioned capital, not diplomatic statements or domestic budget lines alone, is this system's most reliable lever, though even that lever's output (the Upper Trishuli-1 EWS) has an unconfirmed performance record on the one day it mattered.
